# Lost off Scilly

@shot fleet {"mode": "fog"}
In October 1707, a fleet of British warships was sailing home from the Mediterranean, feeling its way through fog and heavy seas toward the English Channel.

For days, cloud had hidden the Sun and stars, so the navigators couldn't take a sighting. Their position was an estimate. The estimate was wrong.

@shot fleet {"mode": "wreck"}
On the night of the twenty second, the flagship Association struck the rocks of the Isles of Scilly, off the tip of Cornwall. Three more ships followed her. Somewhere between fourteen hundred and two thousand sailors drowned, among them the admiral, Sir Cloudesley Shovell.

@shot fleet {"mode": "map"}
Historians still argue about exactly what went wrong: bad charts, currents, errors in reckoning. But the disaster became a symbol of a problem every sailor of the age shared. Even on a clear day, with the best instruments in the world, no one at sea could say how far east or west they were.

@shot titlecard {"title": "Longitude", "sub": "The clock that found the way at sea"}
The answer, when it finally came, didn't come from an astronomer. It came from a village carpenter from the north of England, and a clock.

# Two numbers

@shot latitude {}
A position at sea is two numbers. The first, latitude, is how far north or south you are, and it's the easy one. At noon, measure how high the Sun stands above the horizon, look up a table, and you have it. At night, the height of the Pole Star does the same job.

@shot globe {"stage": "spin"}
Longitude, how far east or west you are, is a different kind of problem. The Earth turns under the sky, so every line of longitude sees the same sky, just at a different time. There's nothing overhead that tells you where you are.

# Longitude is time

@shot globe {"stage": "hours"}
But that's also the way in. The Earth turns three hundred and sixty degrees every twenty four hours. That's fifteen degrees every hour. So a difference in longitude is really a difference in time.

@shot globe {"stage": "noon"}
Suppose you carry a clock set to the time at your home port. Out at sea, you watch for the moment the Sun is highest: local noon. If your clock says it's three in the afternoon back home at that moment, you're three hours behind home. Forty five degrees west.

@shot tolerance {"stage": "prize"}
In 1714, Parliament offered a prize of twenty thousand pounds, a fortune at the time, for any practical method of finding longitude to within half a degree, proven on a voyage to the West Indies.

@shot tolerance {"stage": "math"}
Half a degree of longitude is two minutes of time. A voyage to the West Indies took about six weeks. So a clock would have to stay within two minutes over six weeks. Less than three seconds a day, on a rolling ship.

@shot tolerance {"stage": "rate"}
Strictly, it didn't have to be perfect, only predictable. A clock that always gains exactly ten seconds a day is fine, because you can subtract. What it couldn't do was wander.

@shot pendulum {}
And the best clocks of the day kept time with a pendulum, which is hopeless at sea. Every roll of the deck adds its own push to the swing. Heat makes a pendulum longer and slower, and on the way to the Caribbean, the temperature changes enormously.

@shot lunar {}
Most experts assumed the answer would come from the sky instead. The Moon moves against the stars by about its own width every hour, so in principle it's a clock anyone can read. Measure the angle between the Moon and a bright star, compare it with predicted tables, and you get the time at home.

The trouble was that the Moon's motion is fiendishly complicated. Good enough tables didn't exist until the 1750s, and turning one measurement into a position took a long calculation.

# The carpenter

@shot wood {"stage": "wood"}
John Harrison was born in Yorkshire in 1693, the son of a carpenter, and trained as one himself. He taught himself clockmaking, and his early clocks were made almost entirely of wood.

@shot wood {"stage": "oil"}
He cut the wheels from oak and used lignum vitae, a tropical hardwood so oily that it lubricates itself, for the parts that rub. Clock oil in his day went gummy and stopped clocks, so Harrison simply designed the oil out.

@shot gridiron {}
Then he tackled temperature. His gridiron pendulum uses rods of steel and brass. Brass expands more than steel when it warms, and the rods are arranged so the steel lets the weight down while the brass pushes it back up. With the right lengths, the two cancel, and the pendulum stays the same length.

@shot wood {"stage": "accuracy"}
By the mid-1720s, Harrison claimed his best clocks were keeping time to about a second a month, far better than anything in London. But they still had pendulums.

# Four machines

@shot h1 {"stage": "build"}
In 1730 he went to London with a plan for a clock that could go to sea. The famous clockmaker George Graham lent him money, and five years later he had built H1: a brass machine with no pendulum at all.

@shot h1 {"stage": "balances"}
Instead, two dumbbell-shaped balances rock back and forth, linked so they always move in opposite directions. When the ship lurches, it pushes one balance forward and the other back, and the two effects cancel.

@shot h1 {"stage": "trial"}
In 1736 it went to sea, to Lisbon and back. As England came into sight on the way home, Harrison told the officers that the headland they took for Start Point was really the Lizard, about sixty miles further west. He was right.

@shot h3 {"stage": "strip"}
Harrison wasn't satisfied. He spent more than twenty years on two more sea clocks, and along the way invented two things we still use. The bimetallic strip: two metals bonded together, which bends as the temperature changes. It's still inside thermostats today.

@shot h3 {"stage": "bearing"}
And the caged roller bearing, which lets a shaft turn on rollers held apart in a cage, so they don't rub against each other. You'll find its descendants in almost every machine with a spinning shaft.

@shot h3 {"stage": "fail"}
But the third clock, H3, took him nineteen years, and it never kept time well enough. Large, slow balances were the wrong idea.

# The watch

@shot h4 {"stage": "reveal"}
His fourth timekeeper, finished in 1759, looks almost nothing like the others. H4 is a watch, about thirteen centimetres across. You could hold it in one hand.

@shot h4 {"stage": "balance"}
The secret is speed. Its balance swings five times a second, far faster than the slow balances of the sea clocks. A small, fast balance holds its rhythm, and the motion of a ship barely disturbs it.

@shot h4 {"stage": "pallets"}
Each swing is kept going by tiny pallets cut from diamond, about one millimetre by two, so they hardly wear. And a bimetallic strip adjusts the balance spring as the temperature changes.

@shot h4 {"stage": "fusee"}
To keep the drive steady, H4 uses a fusee, a cone-shaped pulley. When the mainspring is fully wound it pulls hard, so its chain works on the narrow end of the cone. As the spring runs down, the chain moves toward the wide end, where it gets more leverage. The push reaching the balance stays nearly constant.

# The voyages

@shot voyage {"leg": "jamaica"}
In November 1761, H4 sailed for Jamaica aboard HMS Deptford, in the care of Harrison's son, William. The crossing took about nine weeks.

@shot voyage {"leg": "jamaica", "stage": "result"}
When they arrived, William worked out that, after allowing for its rate, the watch was just over five seconds out, eighty one days after it had last been checked. In longitude, that's an error of little more than a mile.

@shot voyage {"leg": "barbados"}
The Board of Longitude, which ran the prize, wasn't satisfied. Harrison hadn't declared the watch's rate in advance, and even Jamaica's own longitude was uncertain. So in 1764, with the rate stated before sailing, H4 went to Barbados. Over forty seven days, its error was thirty nine seconds, about ten miles. Three times better than the prize demanded.

# The prize

@shot board {"stage": "doubt"}
It still wasn't enough. Several members of the Board were astronomers who favoured the lunar method, including Nevil Maskelyne, who became Astronomer Royal in 1765. One watch, they argued, might be luck. The question was whether anyone else could make another.

@shot board {"stage": "disclosure"}
So in 1765 Harrison was paid half the prize, ten thousand pounds, on condition that he took H4 apart in front of a panel of experts, explained every part, and handed it over. The Board then had the watchmaker Larcum Kendall make a copy.

@shot board {"stage": "cook"}
That copy, known as K1, sailed to the Pacific with Captain James Cook on his second voyage, from 1772 to 1775. Cook called it his trusty friend and never-failing guide.

@shot board {"stage": "king"}
Meanwhile Harrison, now in his late seventies, built one more watch, H5. It was tested at King George the Third's observatory at Kew in 1772, and the King took Harrison's side. In 1773, Parliament awarded Harrison a further eight thousand seven hundred and fifty pounds.

@shot board {"stage": "ledger"}
Over the years he'd received more than twenty three thousand pounds, more than the prize itself. But the Board never formally declared that he had won it. He died in 1776.

# Still keeping time

@shot outro {}
Within a few decades, other makers had found simpler, cheaper designs, and marine chronometers were becoming common at sea. The lunar method didn't vanish either. Navigators used both, and each checked the other.

@shot gps {}
And the idea underneath never went away. Your phone finds its position by timing signals from satellites, each one carrying an atomic clock. Finding where you are is still a question of knowing exactly what time it is. That's a story for another video.
